Issue 14

A Texas appeals court held this week that an independent, elder-governed church is entitled to the same constitutional protection from judicial review as a diocese with a code of canon law. Read alongside the death of a 20-year-old in a Southern Baptist missionary boot camp, the week poses one question twice: if the courts will not examine how elders investigate and govern, who will?

SBC & Convention Watch

Annual meeting, entities, ERLC, resolutions, denominational politics.

A student died in Spurgeon College’s mission training program. Ben Speidel, 20, an incoming Spurgeon College student, died Saturday, Aug. 15, during a field training exercise for the college’s Fusion program at Wyandotte County Lake in Kansas. Deputies and paramedics responded to a 7:40 p.m. call; he was pronounced dead at the hospital. Spurgeon — housed within Midwestern Baptist Theological Seminary — confirmed the death Wednesday, with president Jason K. Allen calling it “a tragic, traumatic event for all of us.” Sheriff’s detectives say they do not suspect foul play; cause of death remains undetermined. Fusion, a two-semester program combining boot camp, field exercises, and simulated security crises before three-month IMB deployments, has drawn criticism from some former participants who describe the training as physically and spiritually harmful. Those allegations are contested and unproven, and the investigation is not finished. But two things are true at once: a family is grieving, and a program that markets hardness now has to answer for it. The Roys Report

A searchable database of accused Protestant leaders went live. ProtestantAccountability.org launched Wednesday with roughly 2,700 entries drawn from criminal indictments, lawsuits, and news reports — 1,578 lead and solo pastors, 694 youth pastors and leaders, 172 associate pastors, 93 worship leaders. Most entries include a photo, job titles, and every congregation where the person worked. Accusation is not conviction, and any elder using such a list needs to keep that distinction firmly in hand. What has changed is practical: the “he moved to another state and nobody knew” era is closing. The Roys Report

Christa Brown, twenty years on. Brown’s Aug. 19 opinion piece revisits the letters she sent SBC leaders in August 2006 warning them about clergy abuse, and argues the convention still is not listening. It is an advocate’s piece and reads like one. Read it anyway — the value is in the specificity of what she asked for two decades ago and what was done with it. The Roys Report

Nevada Baptists name Adam Mallette. The Executive Board of the Nevada Baptist Convention voted unanimously Aug. 11 to approve Mallette, lead teaching pastor of King’s Church in Las Vegas, as its next executive director/treasurer. Another state-convention leadership change on the heels of Alabama’s earlier this month. These transitions determine which churches get planted and which pastors get supported for the next decade, and they almost never make national news. Baptist Press

Reformed & Confessional

Did Missouri Presbytery examine its candidate properly? The Heidelblog took up on Aug. 18 the complaint filed after Missouri Presbytery (PCA) approved the ordination of a same-sex-attracted man on April 22, 2025 — a Ruling Elder present at the meeting argued the presbytery’s action violated the PCA’s own standards. Thirteen comments and counting, which is the tell. Baptists have no presbytery to appeal to, which cuts both ways: no external review of a bad ordination, and no external interference in a good one. Worth reading for how an examination is supposed to work. Heidelblog

Culture & Church-State

Religious liberty, ethics, politics affecting the church, cultural engagement.

An elder-governed church gets the same constitutional protection as a diocese. The Fifth Court of Appeals in Dallas ruled Wednesday that the church-autonomy doctrine bars civil courts from hearing most of Cindy and Jerry Clemishire’s claims against Gateway Church, three of its elders, and Robert and Deborah Morris — because adjudicating them would require a court to probe how church leaders investigated allegations and what they concluded. Justice Mike Lee, writing for a panel with Justices Emily Miskel and Nancy Kennedy, leaned on the Texas Supreme Court’s 2021 In re Diocese of Lubbock and wrote the line elders should sit with: “A church governed by a Board of Elders is entitled to the same constitutional protection as is a church governed by a robust code of canon law.” Claims against the Robert Morris Evangelistic Association survive, as do claims arising from a May 30, 2025 affidavit the court found outside the church’s investigation. Morris pleaded guilty last October to abusing Clemishire beginning when she was 12 and served six months. This is the most consequential church-state development of the month for congregational polity: the protection Rome has long enjoyed now plainly extends to independent elder-led churches — and so does the responsibility that comes with being unreviewable. The Roys Report

ERLC honors the daughter of a pastor imprisoned in China. ERLC trustees named Grace Jin Drexel the recipient of the John Leland Religious Liberty Award while her father, Pastor Ezra Jin, was still imprisoned in China for his faith. He has since been freed. Baptist Press reported the presentation Aug. 20. The Leland award is worth knowing about by name — Leland was the Baptist who traded Madison a vote for the First Amendment. Baptist Press

ChinaAid asks the White House to bring John Cao home. Cao was imprisoned seven years by the Chinese Communist Party for his Christian work; advocates say his passport was then confiscated, preventing his return to his American wife and children in North Carolina. ChinaAid petitioned for intervention this week, citing his declining health. A concrete, nameable prayer item for a congregation that has been praying generally for the persecuted church. Baptist Press

Americans are rethinking legalized marijuana. An Aug. 20 Heidelblog item notes 24 states have legalized recreational use since 2012, seven more have decriminalized to varying degrees, and the administration moved state-licensed medical marijuana off Schedule I in April — even as public opinion turns. A pastoral rather than political matter in most congregations: the members affected are already in the room. Heidelblog

Also Worth Noting

Notable controversy and media shake-ups — reported with discernment, not appetite.

Tom Buck is accused of plagiarizing sermons. The Texas pastor and prominent conservative Baptist voice — who has himself publicly condemned sermon plagiarism — faces allegations of “habitual and systemic” borrowing, reported Aug. 20. Notably, allies have rallied to his defense by arguing that the real scandal is who surfaced the accusation. That move is worth watching independently of whether the underlying charge holds up: when the response to evidence is a question about the motives of the person presenting it, the argument has already changed subjects. The Roys Report

ACNA court finds a bishop guilty of misconduct. An Anglican Church in North America ecclesiastical court found the bishop who led a chaplains’ group out of the denomination guilty of causing the breakaway, in a ruling reported Aug. 21. A rare thing to observe from outside: a denomination actually running its discipline process to a verdict on a man who had already left. The Roys Report

Life sentence for an itinerant minister; wife convicted of facilitating. A Tennessee jury found the minister guilty of child rape on Aug. 17; he was sentenced to life without parole, and his wife was convicted of facilitating the abuse. The second conviction is the one to notice. Abuse of this duration is rarely a solo act — it is sustained by adults who chose not to see. The Roys Report

$335,000 allegedly rerouted from an Arizona church. A former administrative assistant faces multiple felony charges after detectives alleged she moved more than $335,000 into personal accounts. Filed under the recurring lesson: the person who reconciles the bank statement should not be the person who writes the checks, and an elder board that cannot say who does each should fix that this month. The Roys Report

For the Elder’s Desk

A pastoral takeaway for your leadership team this week.

The Dallas court gave elder-led churches something significant this week, and it is worth being honest about what it is. It is not a finding that Gateway acted well. The court explicitly declined to decide whether Gateway’s statements were defamatory. What it decided is that no civil court may look — that when a board of elders investigates one of its own and then tells the congregation what it found, the process is ecclesiastical and the courthouse door is closed. Rome has had that protection for a long time. Now, in Texas at least, so do we. Set that beside the second story of the week: a twenty-year-old dead after a training exercise in a program whose critics were dismissed, whose defenders are already saying these things just happen, and whose culture teaches young men to recite that suffering is expected. Both stories run on the same fuel — an institution’s confidence that its own internal judgment is sufficient. The court has now told us it will not check our work. That is not a gift; it is the removal of a backstop. Which means the question for your board is not are we protected but what would we have caught. So: when your elders last investigated a complaint against one of your own — not a hypothetical, the actual last time — who ran it, who saw the evidence, what was written down, and would any of it survive a stranger reading it cold? If no one on the board can answer without checking with someone else, the answer is that your process is a memory rather than a practice. Paul required that a charge against an elder be heard on two or three witnesses and that those who persist in sin be rebuked in the presence of all (1 Tim. 5:19–20). He gave the church a procedure precisely because he assumed no one else would supply one.
